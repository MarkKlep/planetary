Planetary · Brand

Marks and cover for the LinkedIn page

All three are drawn from the app itself rather than around it — the amber of --signal is the one point light every position in the scene is computed from, and every disc here is lit by it. Pick a mark and a cover; the files are listed at the bottom.

The constraint that decides it

LinkedIn shows an organisation logo large exactly once — on the page header. Everywhere else that matters (the feed, search results, the "pages you may know" rail) it is about 48 px square. Judge these at the small end of the ladder, not the big end.

Same three shapes as public/favicon.svg and the same tokens as variables.scss — the amber star, one orbit, one body on it. Three things the 16 px favicon had no room for are added, and each is a fact about the app rather than a flourish: the body carries a terminator lit from the star's real direction, the orbit ramps dim-to-bright into the body the way iss-trajectory.ts draws one, and the star is limb-darkened from the centre out rather than shaded from one side — the correction planet-icons.tsx already makes, since a dark edge is a terminator and that is the one thing a star cannot have.

Works because
Continuous with the favicon already in the tab, so the two read as one brand. Holds its shape down to 36 px.
Costs you
An orbit with a dot on it is a well-worn shape. What separates it here is the amber star and the phase — both of which survive the small sizes.

Ringed

Unmistakable at any size.

The ring system is the one structure in the project that is genuinely one-dimensional: rings.ts builds it from measured boundary radii and Cassini occultation optical depths, so the bands and the gap between them sit in roughly their real proportions, brightest at the B ring. The dark band across the globe is the rings' own shadow — the half of the pair a shadow map cannot do, which saturn.ts patches into the planet's shader.

Works because
The clearest read of the three at feed size. Nothing else is shaped like a ringed planet.
Costs you
Drops the amber entirely, so it shares nothing with the favicon. And a ringed planet is the most-used mark in this whole subject area.

Three orbits rather than one, for what the app really is — a whole solar system on a single clock, every body a pure function of it. The outer body keeps the terminator and the ramp; the two inner ones are plain dots, which is how body-marker.ts draws anything that has fallen below a few pixels. At true scale that is most of this scene, most of the time.

Works because
Says solar system, where the other two say a planet. The most honest description of the product.
Costs you
Falls apart under about 56 px — which is exactly the size LinkedIn uses in the feed and in search results. That is disqualifying for this particular job.

Cover image

And the banner across the top

1128 × 191 — LinkedIn's cover size, and a 5.9:1 frame that very little survives. Both cuts keep the left quarter quiet, because that is where the organisation logo is overlaid and the sides are the first thing a narrow viewport crops.

Untyped

Recommended

No wordmark. LinkedIn already prints the name underneath.

The orbit is seen edge-on — not a stylisation but what a solar system looks like from inside its own ecliptic, and the one composition that fits a 5.9:1 frame without cropping anything. The far half of the orbit is drawn behind the star and occluded by it, so the ellipse reads as depth rather than as a flat oval. The line ramps into the body and the body carries a terminator, exactly as in the marks above.

Why
LinkedIn sets the organisation name in large type directly beneath the cover. Repeating it inside the cover is the most common way these end up looking cluttered.

With wordmark

Same system, shifted right, wordmark in the clear left half.

Set in the IBM Plex Sans Condensed the app's chrome uses, with the font embedded in the SVG so it renders identically anywhere. The tagline is Plex Mono, letter-spaced, in the same --ink-faint the nav panel uses for its eyebrows.

Why
Use this one if the page will be linked from places that crop or re-host the cover without the name attached.

  • Every mark is full-bleed square with the artwork inset from the edges, so LinkedIn can round the corners in whichever surface it likes without clipping anything.
  • The dark ground is part of the mark, not a placeholder — it is --void, the same colour the app paints before the first frame. There is no transparent-background variant, and a logo of a night sky does not want one.
  • The banner's wordmark cut has the font embedded in the SVG, so it renders identically wherever it is opened — no missing-font fallback.
  • Both banners carry a trace of grain. It is not a texture effect: a dark gradient this wide crosses only a handful of 8-bit levels and quantises into contour bands that read as vertical seams. The noise dithers them out.
